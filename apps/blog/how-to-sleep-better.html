How to Actually Sleep Better (Without Buying Anything)

Better sleep doesn't require a £200 pillow or a sleep tracking ring. Here's what actually works — and it's all free.

📅 July 2026 ⏱ 5 min read ✦ Wellness & Habits
🌙

The sleep wellness industry is worth billions. There's a weighted blanket for every budget, a supplement stack for every sleep problem, and approximately one thousand podcast episodes about why you're not sleeping well enough and what you need to buy to fix it.

Here's the thing. Most of what actually improves sleep costs nothing. It's just not as easy to sell.

This isn't a post about magnesium or melatonin or the perfect mattress. It's about the small, free, unsexy things that genuinely work — backed by sleep science, not sponsored content.

Why you're probably not sleeping as badly as you think

Before anything else: most people overestimate how little sleep they're getting.

When you lie awake at 3am convinced you haven't slept, you've often been in light sleep for chunks of that time. The brain is bad at tracking its own unconsciousness. This matters because sleep anxiety — the worry about not sleeping — is one of the biggest drivers of actual insomnia. The fix for some people isn't better sleep habits. It's stopping the catastrophising about sleep.

If you regularly get 5–6 hours and feel fine, you might just be a short sleeper. If you feel exhausted, read on.

01

The temperature thing is real

Your body needs to drop its core temperature to fall asleep — by about 1–2°C. This is why a cool room helps, why a warm bath before bed actually works (it draws heat to the surface and helps your core cool faster), and why summer nights are harder. The sweet spot for most people is somewhere between 16–19°C. If you run hot, a fan is more useful than any sleep supplement.

02

Light in the morning matters more than dark at night

Everyone talks about blue light at night. Fewer people talk about the other side of the equation: bright light in the morning is one of the most powerful sleep regulators that exists. Morning light — ideally natural, ideally within an hour of waking — sets your body's internal clock. It tells your system when the day starts, which then predicts when melatonin will rise 14–16 hours later. Get outside in the morning, even briefly, even on a cloudy day, and your body will find its rhythm more naturally than any evening routine can force.

03

Your wake time matters more than your bedtime

Most sleep advice focuses on when you go to bed. Sleep science focuses on when you wake up. A consistent wake time — even at weekends, even after a bad night — is the anchor your circadian rhythm organises itself around. It feels counterintuitive when you're tired, but sleeping in after a rough night often makes the next night worse by shifting your sleep pressure and delaying your body clock. Pick a wake time you can actually stick to. That's it. That's the single most impactful sleep habit most people aren't doing.

04

Stop trying to sleep

This one sounds like a riddle but it's genuinely evidence-based. A technique called sleep restriction therapy — developed by sleep researchers, not wellness influencers — works by rebuilding your association between bed and sleep. The core principle: only go to bed when you're actually sleepy (not just tired and watching TV), and get out of bed if you're lying awake for more than 20 minutes. Lying in bed awake for hours teaches your brain that bed is a place for wakefulness. Getting up, doing something calm and non-stimulating, and going back when genuinely sleepy gradually rewires that. It feels awful for the first week. It works.

05

The last hour actually matters — but not for the reason you think

The point of a wind-down routine isn't to trick yourself into sleep. It's to create a psychological buffer between the stimulation of the day and the stillness of the night. That buffer can be whatever works for you. A walk. A shower. Reading something that isn't work. Spark Quotes instead of a scroll. The medium doesn't matter as much as the intention — a deliberate transition that signals to your nervous system that the day is done. What doesn't work as a buffer: anxious to-do lists in your head, heated conversations, anything that activates problem-solving. Your brain will keep working on it after lights out.

06

Caffeine has a longer half-life than you think

Caffeine has a half-life of about 5–6 hours, meaning that coffee at 3pm still has half its effect at 8–9pm. For some people, especially those who metabolise caffeine slowly, the effect lasts even longer. The standard advice — cut off caffeine by 2pm — actually holds up. If you're a slow metaboliser (you feel coffee strongly, it keeps you wired for hours), noon is a more realistic cutoff. This isn't about never having a late coffee. It's about knowing that your 4pm latte might be doing more than you think.

07

Alcohol is not a sleep aid

It feels like one because it's sedating. But alcohol suppresses REM sleep — the restorative, dreaming stage — and causes more fragmented sleep in the second half of the night. The grogginess the morning after isn't just dehydration. It's disrupted sleep architecture. If you drink regularly and wonder why you're tired despite getting "enough" hours, this is likely part of the picture.

What to actually try first

If you're going to take one thing from this:

"Set a consistent wake time and stick to it for two weeks."

Not a perfect bedtime. Not a new supplement. Just the same alarm, every day, including Saturday. Give it a fortnight and see what shifts.

Everything else — the temperature, the morning light, the wind-down — supports that anchor. But the anchor comes first.

A note on real sleep problems

If you've tried the basics and still can't sleep — if it's been months, if it's affecting your mental health and daily function — please talk to your GP. Cognitive Behavioural Therapy for Insomnia (CBT-I) is the gold-standard evidence-based treatment and is often available on the NHS. It works better than medication for most people and the effects last longer.

This post is for the person who's sleeping badly and suspects their habits are part of it. If it's beyond habits, get proper support.
